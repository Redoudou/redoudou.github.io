Running an organization with agents

How to connect information, coordinate work and keep people responsible for decisions.

By Redwan Meslem

Running an organization means keeping many threads moving at once: relationships, meetings, communications, projects and follow-up. At the Enterprise Ethereum Alliance, my work spans membership, events, research and working groups. Much of the coordination depends on carrying context from one activity into the next.

A conversation can lead to a research question, an introduction or a new project. The operational challenge is to capture that connection, decide what matters and make sure someone follows through. That is where agents can be useful.

Start with the work

Before introducing an agent, define the recurring process. What starts it? What information does it need? What should be ready at the end? Who owns the next step?

For a membership renewal, the useful outcome might be a short account brief and a draft message for the relationship owner. For an event, it might be a confirmed participant list and a follow-up plan. A clear outcome gives the workflow a boundary and makes its usefulness easier to judge.

Give the organization a shared memory

Relevant context is usually spread across email, meeting notes, contact records and documents. Bringing it together starts with agreeing which record to trust, how to identify the same person or organization across systems, and where decisions should be recorded.

An agent needs to distinguish a confirmed decision from a suggestion, and a current record from an outdated one. Keeping sources attached to a brief lets the person reviewing it check the reasoning and correct mistakes.

Connect activities into workflows

Consider the work around a member meeting. Beforehand, an agent could prepare the history of the relationship and open questions. Afterwards, it could extract proposed actions from the notes, prepare record updates and draft a follow-up. A person checks the interpretation and decides what to send.

The same pattern applies to research: collect relevant publications, compare them with existing work, prepare a briefing and bring it to an editor. Each step should leave a useful record for the next one.

Keep ownership clear

Agents can gather, compare and prepare. People remain responsible for priorities, relationships and commitments. Every workflow needs a named owner, a clear review point and a way to handle uncertainty.

A draft should remain visibly a draft until reviewed. If a source is missing or two records disagree, the workflow should surface that question. Access should also fit the task: preparing a briefing does not require permission to send messages or change financial records.

Review the operation, not just the output

A useful operating rhythm includes checking what finished, what failed and what still needs a decision. Track whether follow-ups happen, records stay current and people spend less time reconstructing context. The number of agents or generated documents says little about whether the organization is working better.

Start with one recurring process, review its results and fix the weak handoffs before expanding. The aim is an organization that remembers what it agreed, knows who owns the next step and reliably moves work forward.
